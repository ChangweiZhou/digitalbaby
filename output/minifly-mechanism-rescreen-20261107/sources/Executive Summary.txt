Executive Summary
We assessed eleven biologically inspired circuit motifs for their fit to a tiny lifelong learner. Insect circuits
emerge strongest: the Drosophila central complex (including a compass ring and vector memory) and the
mushroom‐body output loop already present in their model show clear evidence and match the size
constraints. Classic brain systems (hippocampus/neocortex, basal ganglia, cerebellum) have solid
experimental backing but imply large recurrent networks or many synapses, making them heavyweight.
Others (cortical feedback for credit assignment, thalamocortical loops, attention gating) are mostly
proposals or hypotheses with less direct evidence and may require dense recurrence or modulatory
signals, leaving their utility uncertain. In particular, only the basal‐ganglia and mushroom‐body loops have
well‐characterized local three‐factor plasticity supporting their function. Sleep-dependent consolidation and
grid/head‐direction attractors offer known mechanisms (replay, path integration) but their circuits are large
or abstract. Overall, insect‐inspired modules top the list, followed by hippocampus/neocortex and actor-
critic BG. Our detailed blocks below justify each entry and propose minimal computational tests. The final
table ranks each by evidence strength, parameter size, plastic-rule plausibility, and test cost.


Hippocampus–Neocortex (Complementary Learning Systems). What it computes: The hippocampus
rapidly encodes new episodes and “replays” them to train the slow-learning neocortex, which gradually
abstracts common structure (inferred from lesion and modeling studies【9†L19-L27】【10†L67-L75】). This
complementary system explains temporally graded retrograde amnesia (new memories depend on
hippocampus, old ones on cortex【9†L68-L74】【10†L61-L68】) and fast binding of arbitrary associations
(shown by hippocampal lesion deficits【9†L68-L74】). McClelland et al. 1995 famously formalized this idea.


Wiring diagram: The hippocampal circuit is trisynaptic: entorhinal cortex (EC) → dentate gyrus (DG) → CA3 →
CA1 → subiculum/EC, with DG performing pattern separation (divergent ≈1000:1 DG granule:EC input
【5†L79-L87】) and CA3 recurrent excitatory attractors (each CA3 cell receives ~1000 mossy‐fiber inputs
from DG【5†L81-L89】). CA1 outputs back to EC and cortex. Neocortical areas project densely to EC and
hippocampus (millions of inputs) and receive backpropagating signals during sharp-wave ripples. The
convergence ratio is high (many cortical neurons to single hippocampal pyramidal cells) and vice versa
during replay.


Fixed vs plastic: Hippocampal feedforward and recurrent synapses are highly plastic (LTP/LTD in DG–CA3 and
CA3–CA3【5†L57-L65】). The DG→CA3 “mossy fiber” to pyramidal synapses can potentiate quickly, while
CA3 recurrent synapses implement autoassociative LTP【5†L55-L64】. Cortico-hippocampal synapses are
also plastic with similar Hebbian rules. Hippocampal plasticity is gated by novelty and neuromodulators, but
essentially every coactive pre-post pairing in CA3 under a modulatory signal induces weight change.
Neocortical synapses change far more slowly (during sleep replay). The local update in the hippocampus
can be expressed as: Δw = learning_rate × presynaptic_activity × postsynaptic_activity (for potentiation,
with an optional *-sign for depression), gated by a “novelty/error” signal (e.g. acetylcholine) each time a
pattern is encoded or replayed. In cortex, plasticity per event is weak.


Free parameters: Dominated by hippocampal synapses. If N_c cortex cells project to N_h hippocampal cells,
and K DG cells per hippocampal cell, free weights ≈N_c×N_h (cortex→EC/hipp) + N_DG×N_CA3 (DG→CA3) +

N_CA3^2 (recurrent) + N_CA3×N_CA1 + N_CA1×N_c. For a small network (e.g. 10^3 cortex ×10^3
hippocampus), this is ~10^6–10^7 parameters. Neocortex can have billions, but after sharing by cell-type
connections (if all CA3→CA3 synapses are one “type”) it is still large. Thus order 10^5–10^7, depending on
hippocampal size and representational width.


Models: Norm→O’Reilly 2003 (Psych Rev) formalized CLS; O’Reilly & Frank 2006 (Neural Comput) included
hippocampal recall in an RL agent. Davachi 2004 J Neurosci provided evidence for rapid hippocampal
plasticity. More recently, Schapiro et al. (2026) reviewed hippocampal structure learning【2†L14-L18】
(though we did not open it, it’s a commentary). Large-scale deep learning “memory” models (e.g. RNN with
fast weights) are conceptually similar but not directly tested. The key successes are slow/faster learning and
replay consolidation; failures include difficulty pinpointing biological local learning rules beyond the
concept.


Benefit to a small learner: Primarily retention of old tasks and reuse/generalization. The hippocampal
store prevents new learning from overwriting old (avoiding catastrophic interference)【9†L30-L34】, and
replay during offline periods trains the cortex, aiding future generalization to unseen inputs.
Mechanistically, novel inputs are rapidly stored (allowing one-shot binding of arbitrary combos) while slow
replay weeds out noise. The interplay means newly learned rules become encoded in cortex only after
multiple re-exposures, aligning with the need to combine them with prior structure【9†L25-L34】. Thus one
expects improved long-term memory and sample efficiency via unsupervised interleaving (though explicit
credit assignment on unseen inputs is indirect).


Minimal test: Compare a dual-memory network versus one homogenous net on an interleaved learning task.
E.g. teach half of examples in one context, then test with new examples of the same class. One network has
a separate fast “episodic” module that replays to a slow net; the control does not. The metric is
generalization accuracy on the novel half. A significant advantage in the dual-system suggests
complementary learning benefits. Parameter count would be matched by limiting hidden units.


Confidence: High. The CLS idea is supported by many lesion and recording studies (graded amnesia【9†L68-
L74】【10†L61-L68】) and extensive modeling【9†L19-L27】. The main question is how cortex learns
without interfering, but that is precisely the hypothesis. Biological plasticity (LTP) exists as required【5†L91-
L100】.



Prefrontal Cortex + Basal Ganglia (Working-Memory Gating). What it computes: A learned gating
mechanism for working memory: the basal ganglia (BG) learn which prefrontal cortex (PFC) representations
are task-relevant and disinhibit those to update, while suppressing others【61†L53-L60】【20†L9-L16】. In
effect, PFC maintains sustained (bistable) representations, and BG “Go/NoGo” pathways control when to
load or ignore new inputs. This is inferred from computational models (O’Reilly & Frank 2006) and
supported by human/monkey imaging. Saalmann et al. (2012) showed pulvinar (a thalamic BG target)
modulates cortical synchrony with attention, but more directly McNab & Klingberg (2007) found that
prefrontal and basal‐ganglia activity predicts filtering of distractors (Fig.1B–E)【20†L9-L16】, consistent with
BG gating.


Wiring diagram: PFC columns (“stripes”) have recurrent excitatory/inhibitory loops for maintenance. Cortex
projects excitatorily to striatal medium spiny neurons in the direct (“Go”) and indirect (“NoGo”) pathways




                                                       2
【61†L63-L71】. The direct pathway (via D1-expressing MSNs) inhibits the BG output nuclei (SNr/GPi),
disinhibiting a thalamic target (often mediodorsal thalamus), which then excites PFC to allow an update. The
indirect pathway (D2 neurons → GPe → STN → GPi) has the opposite effect. Dopamine from VTA/SNc
modulates these: bursts (positive RPE) strengthen Go-pathway learning, dips strengthen NoGo learning. A
diffuse Achergic (or cortical ACh from nucleus basalis) signal can globally open/close WM. Convergence:
each striatal neuron receives inputs from ~10^4 cortical cells, and each BG output integrates many MSNs.
The BG-to-thalamus inhibition is massive (divergent), enabling a small Go signal to open a channel.


Fixed vs plastic: Corticostriatal synapses are highly plastic via three‐factor rules: presynaptic cortex activity,
postsynaptic striatum activity, and dopamine. Δw for a D1 synapse is positive if pre×post is high and DA is
above baseline; for D2 it is reversed (LTD). We can write, in words: Δw = learning_rate × presyn_activity ×
postsyn_activity × (DA_signal)【61†L59-L64】, with DA_signal positive for rewards and negative for
punishments. The BG output synapses and PFC recurrent loops are largely fixed (their gating function
comes from architecture). The modulatory DA and ACh signals broadcast globally.


Free parameters: Predominantly cortico-striatal weights. If there are N_P PFC neurons and N_S striatal
neurons, roughly N_P×N_S parameters for corticostriatal connections. For a small model, say 100 PFC×100
striatal, this is ~10^4 parameters. Including both direct and indirect cells doubles it, plus a few hundred BG
internal synapses. So on the order of 10^4–10^5. (If weight sharing is applied per neuron type, count is per
type pair but remains ~N_P×N_S.)


Models: The PBWM (prefrontal-basal-ganglia working memory) model by O’Reilly & Frank (Neural
Computation 2006) explicitly implements this, mastering tasks like the 1-2-AX. Joel et al. (Neural Comput.
2002) reviewed BG as actor-critic. Bennett et al. 2021 (Nat. Commun.) modeled prediction-error learning in
the Drosophila MB similarly, using the BG as critic. Izhikevich (Cereb Cortex 2007) showed a spiking network
solving “distal reward” via DA-STDP. Those models get credit assignment and gating right; shortcomings
include scaling to complex state spaces.


Benefit to a small learner: Mainly local credit assignment and multi-step computation. The BG’s RPE
signals solve temporal credit assignment without backprop: each WM update is reinforced by future reward.
This can speed learning (sample efficiency) in tasks needing gated memory (O’Reilly & Frank, 2006). For
example, learning a context-dependent rule (1-2-AX) is efficient with BG gating, whereas a plain RNN learns
slowly. BG gating also implicitly protects old tasks (retention) by only gating when needed. It may not
directly improve rule reuse on unseen inputs, but it enables hierarchical/temporal dependencies to be
learned.


Minimal test: Implement a simple reinforcement task (e.g. alternating context cues) with two networks: one
with a BG-like gating module (trained with DA signals) and one with the same capacity but no gating. Both
have 60k parameters. Compare learning speed or final reward on a delayed-response or context task (e.g.
sequential decision tasks). A significant learning boost or fewer reward drops for the gated model indicates
its utility.


Confidence: High. The actor-critic model of BG and dopaminergic RPE is very well supported (Schultz et al.
1997) and elaborated by many models【61†L99-L107】. The specific mapping to WM gating is model-driven
but bolstered by human fMRI and monkey data (Klingberg 2007) showing striatal-PFC interactions【20†L9-
L16】.

Cortical Feedback (Error/Target Signals). What it computes: Hypothesized credit-assignment signals.
Rather than being directly shown, these are proposed mechanisms: feedback projections (e.g. from higher
to lower cortical areas) could carry error or target information to enable local learning【26†L68-L74】.
Theories include feedback alignment (Lillicrap et al. 2016), predictive coding (Rao & Ballard 1999), target
propagation, and especially apical dendritic error signals (review: Guerguiev et al. 2017, Payeur et al. 2021).
A recent experimental advance (Francioni et al., 2026) found evidence of “vectorized instructive signals” in
cortical apical dendrites during learning. Thus, while computationally attractive, this is mostly proposed/
inferred.


Wiring diagram: Cortical layers form a bi-directional hierarchy. Pyramidal neurons in layers 2/3 send
feedforward outputs; layer 5/6 send feedforward to subcortical targets (e.g. thalamus, brainstem). Feedback
connections from higher areas target distal apical dendrites of lower-layer pyramidal neurons (often via
layer 1) and layer 5. These feedback synapses are excitatory. Inhibitory interneurons (somatostatin- and VIP-
type) modulate dendritic integration. Often, each neuron receives convergent feedback from multiple
higher-area neurons (divergent ratio many-to-1). Feedback can also come via the thalamus (e.g. pulvinar
loops). Pathway signs are glutamatergic (excitatory); local disinhibitory circuits can gate their effect. No
simple ratio is known; connectivity is widespread and overlapping.


Fixed vs plastic: Both feedforward and feedback synapses are plastic (cortical LTP/LTD exist in both
directions), but specifics of plasticity rules are debated. Feedback synapses might be more modulatory (e.g.
NMDA-dependent coincidence detectors). A prime idea is that apical dendrites detect a teaching signal: for
example, if apical input deviates from somatic output, local Ca^2+ spikes can trigger synaptic changes.
Thus, in one model Δw_feedforward ∝ feedforward_pre × (apical_error_signal) (Payeur et al. 2021). In words:
weight change = learning_rate × presyn_activity × (difference between apical instructive input and somatic
output). Often a scalar neuromodulator (e.g. noradrenaline/acetylcholine) might gate plasticity. It is unclear
if feedback weights themselves adapt during a task; many theories assume a fixed feedback weight or
random projection. One can state a generic local rule: Δw = learning_rate × pre × post when the apical
dendrite receives a strong teaching input (Francioni et al. 2026).


Free parameters: If a network has N neurons per layer and full reciprocal connections, the number of
feedback weights is ~N^2 between successive layers. For a modest cortical model with e.g. 100×100, that is
10^4. With L layers, about L×10^4. If feedback is random fixed, those parameters are “free” but not learned.
After sharing by cell type, it might reduce (e.g. one average feedback gain per cell type). Overall, order
10^4–10^5 parameters, depending on layer sizes and whether each feedback synapse is considered
independent.


Models: Many theoretical studies: Lillicrap et al. (2016, PNAS) demonstrated feedback alignment (random
fixed feedback suffices to train a network). Guerguiev et al. (Science 2017) and Sacramento et al. (PLOS
Comput. Biol. 2018) built multi-compartment neuron models that propagate errors to basal and apical
compartments. Payeur et al. (eLife 2021) showed spike-based local learning approximating backprop with
dendrites. These models can learn deep-net tasks (MNIST, image recognition) with only local 3-factor rules,
but often underperform true backprop, and their biological realism is arguable. There are also many
predictive-coding network models (Rao & Ballard 1999; Spratling 2017). Code is available for some (e.g.
Feedback Alignment implementations). These capture general learning but may fail on highly nonlocal
credit assignment tasks.




                                                       4
Benefit to a small learner: Potentially credit assignment with local rules and multi-step computation. If
cortical feedback indeed carries error, a network could learn hierarchical tasks without backprop, enabling
deeper composition of features. In practice, this could improve learning on tasks where hidden layers must
self-organize (e.g. hierarchical vision). Mechanistically, feedback provides a teaching signal to adjust earlier
layers so as to reduce prediction errors (predictive coding) or align feedforward weights (feedback
alignment). It might also enable iterative “inner rehearsal” of predictions (via loops), aiding planning.
However, these advantages depend on feedback being tuned to the task; random or fixed feedback may
only help roughly.


Minimal test: Implement a small multilayer net on a classification problem (e.g. parity or 2-bit rule). Compare
(a) local learning with symmetrical feedback weights (“sign-symmetric backprop”) vs (b) no feedback (or
random feedback). Both have ~60k parameters. Evaluate final accuracy or learning speed. If (a) outperforms
(b), it shows local error signals can substitute for backprop. One could also test with and without apical-
trace gating (e.g. add vs remove a scalar modulatory gating signal) to see impact.


Confidence: Medium. The existence of cortical feedback is unquestioned anatomically, and some form of
predictive signal is plausible (Rao & Ballard 1999), but direct evidence it carries error is indirect. Recent
dendritic imaging (Francioni et al., 2026) supports the concept of apical instructive signals【22†L0-L4】. Still,
translating this to full credit assignment remains speculative.



Cerebellum (Supervised Learning). What it computes: The cerebellum implements a high-dimensional
supervised learning module for precise timing and calibration (known from motor learning, e.g. adaptation,
conditioned reflexes). It performs error-driven learning: climbing fibers signal errors, and Purkinje cells
adjust synapses to minimize future error (shown by countless experiments【28†L48-L56】). The sparse
expansion in the granule-cell layer creates feature space for generalization. Classical evidence: lesions cause
ataxia; blocking LTP/LTD at PF-Purkinje synapses impairs learning (Ito 1982 review). The “computes”
statement: it maps context/mossy-fiber inputs to motor outputs with error correction (converging to an
optimal predictor).


Wiring diagram: Mossy fibers (from cortex, brainstem) synapse onto granule cells (excitatory). Granule cells
bifurcate into parallel fibers, diverging massively (each granule axon contacts ~200k Purkinje cells). A single
Purkinje cell receives ~100k parallel fiber inputs (divergent fan-in)【29†L213-L222】. Each Purkinje cell
(inhibitory) projects to deep cerebellar nuclei (DCN). Climbing fibers from the inferior olive (each climbing
fiber innervates one Purkinje, but is an error-broadcast to ~10 Purkinjes) provide teaching signals.
Interneurons (Golgi cells) provide feedback inhibition in the granule layer; basket/stellate cells inhibit
Purkinje. DCN neurons integrate Purkinje (inhibitory) and mossy collaterals, and send outputs to motor
areas. The net effect is a giant sparse code: ~10^6 granule cells to <10^4 Purkinje in mammals; in a mini-
model perhaps thousands-to-tens.


Fixed vs plastic: The parallel-fiber→Purkinje synapses are plastic: a climbing-fiber spike paired with parallel
fiber activity induces LTD (or LTP)【29†L232-L240】. We can express: Δw_pf-pc = -η × pf_activity ×
CF_signal (assuming CF signal =1 for error event, minus sign for LTD). DCN synapses (mossy→DCN,
Purkinje→DCN) also show plasticity for long-term adaptation. Golgi and basket circuits may be relatively
fixed or exhibit homeostatic plasticity. Plasticity is gated by the climbing fiber (often a one-spike “teacher”
after an error). The learning rule is essentially: if parallel fiber i and Purkinje are coactive when a climbing fiber
bursts, then decrease w_i. Achieved by calcium signals.

Free parameters: The count is huge if every PF-PC synapse is free. In a small network, if there are N_g
granule cells and N_Pur Purkinje cells, PF→PK weights = N_g×N_Pur. For example, if 10,000 granule cells and
100 Purkinje, that's 10^6. We share weights per synapse only by cell type (so no sharing), so free
parameters ~N_gN_Pur. Real cerebellum is on order 10^11 parameters, but an artificial model could compress.
Even a modest granule pool (10^3) with tens of Purkinje yields ~10^4–10^5 weights. Thus order 10^4–10^6*.
(Deep nuclei output weights add thousands more, but smaller scale.)


Models: Marr/Albus (1969–1971) proposed the framework. More recent: Yamazaki & Tanaka (2007 J Neurosci)
modeled cerebellar timing; Dean & Porrill 2010 (Curr Biol) reviewed adaptive filter. Computational models
with code include “CerebellarNet” by Morgan & Sollich (GitHub above) running classic tasks (eyeblink
conditioning, VOR adaptation)【29†L238-L247】. Zhou et al. 2026 (PLOS Comp Biol) implemented a
cerebellum-inspired network for classification (Fashion-MNIST) achieving ~90% accuracy, showing rapid
supervised learning with sparsity【28†L75-L84】. These models succeed at pattern separation and fast
adaptation, but often lack a generic RL credit signal beyond the explicit error. Some gap exists in modeling
chaotic motor dynamics, but overall they capture precise timing and sample-efficient learning.


Benefit to a small learner: Mainly sample efficiency and multi-step calculation of analog transformations.
The cerebellum can learn complex input–output mappings quickly from few error signals (due to huge
expansion). For our learner, adding a cerebellar module would accelerate learning of temporal or
motorizable rules (e.g. arithmetic carried out by timed pulses) and improve retention of fine-grained
adjustments (because errors explicitly train weights). Mechanism: the sparse granule code generalizes
structure, while error clamping ensures convergence. It might not directly yield rule reuse for unseen
patterns (since it is supervised on each error), but it greatly speeds learning of any rule once a training
signal is provided.


Minimal test: Give the network a temporal prediction task (e.g. predict the next element in a sequence after
a short delay). Compare with and without a “cerebellum” expansion module plus supervised error gating.
Measure how fast it learns accurate predictions or motor outputs. The one metric is the error reduction over
trials. A much faster decline with the cerebellum indicates its benefit. Ensure both networks have ~60k total
weights.


Confidence: High. The cerebellar supervised architecture is one of the best-understood; the existence of PF-
LTD via climbing fibers (Ito 1982 review) and known expansion coding【29†L215-L223】 make it
experimentally grounded. The main uncertainty is whether a similar module suits abstract cognitive rules
(likely only for timing/transform tasks).



Basal Ganglia as Actor–Critic (Dopaminergic RPE). What it computes: Reinforcement learning via reward-
prediction error. The ventral striatum/ventral tegmental DA system learns state values (critic), while dorsal
striatum learns action policies (actor)【61†L99-L107】. Dopamine signals (phasic bursts) encode reward
prediction error (observed in monkey SNc; Schultz et al. 1997). This shown by electrophysiology (DA
neurons fire to unexpected rewards) and by the necessity of DA for learning (DA blockers prevent
conditioning). The BG circuits implement three-factor learning: eligibility of cortical-striatal synapses
combined with DA reinforcement (supported by slice experiments, Reynolds & Wickens 2002).


Wiring diagram: Cortex (especially frontal and sensorimotor) projects to the striatum (glutamatergic).
Striatum (D1 “Go” MSNs) projects via the direct pathway to the GPi/SNr (GABAergic), disinhibiting thalamus;



                                                       6
D2 “NoGo” MSNs project via GPe/STN to GPi (inhibitory effect). GPi outputs to thalamus (ventral anterior/
ventral lateral nuclei), which then excite cortex or brainstem. SNc/VTA dopamine neurons project diffusely to
striatum (modulatory). There are roughly 10 cortical inputs per striatal neuron (convergence) and each GPi
neuron integrates thousands of MSNs. Feedback: striosomes to DA as value estimate (actor-critic loop)
【61†L115-L123】.


Fixed vs plastic: The key plastic sites are corticostriatal synapses. These implement TD learning: Δw ∝
presyn_activity × DA_RPE × eligibility_trace. In words, each cortical input that was active just before a phasic
DA “error” is potentiated or depressed. Formally: Δw = η × presynaptic_activity × postsynaptic_activity ×
(DA_signal)【61†L63-L71】, where DA_signal is positive for positive RPE, negative for negative RPE. The
postsynaptic factor (MSN firing) ensures Hebbian-like specificity (with eligibility, Izhikevich 2007). SNc-DA
release (global scalar) gates all such updates. BG internal synapses (striatum-internal, STN-GPe, etc.) are
largely fixed in learning models. There is also dopamine-modulated plasticity in the cortex under BFAs
modulators, but that is slower.


Free parameters: If N_C cortical state units and N_A action-striatum units (both Go and NoGo populations),
weights are N_C×N_A. For a small network (e.g. 100 state units, 10 actions), 10^3 weights. Plus a similar
number for indirect path. So on the order of 10^3–10^4. Each weight is independent (sharing only by cell
class), so scaling directly with problem dimension. (State dimension and action count are the main factors.)


Models: A classic is Frank et al. (J. Cog. Neurosci. 2005) modeling BG as actor-critic in decision tasks. Joel et
al. (Neural Comput. 2002) formalized BG learning as RL. Zheng et al. (Nature Comms 2021) modeled DA
RPE-driven MB learning analogously. In robotics, Kasess et al. (Front. Comp. Neurosci. 2019) used BG
circuits for navigation. Most relevant is Bennett et al. 2021 (Nat Comms) who implemented MB with
reinforcement signals. These models generally get correct graded learning and reward-driven behavior;
they may fail on hierarchical long-horizon planning unless combined with additional structures.


Benefit to a small learner: Critical for credit assignment in reinforcement tasks and sample efficiency on
delayed reward tasks. The dopaminergic RPE bridges delays via eligibility traces, allowing a neuron to
strengthen synapses that were active seconds before a reward (solving the “distal reward problem”【34†L1-
L7】). For our learner, BG-like DA learning could let it learn value functions and gating policies without
backpropagation through time. Mechanism: actions (or memory updates) that led to reward are reinforced
automatically. This also helps retention, since learning is conditional on external reward rather than
overwriting all connections. It can indirectly improve rule reuse by encoding abstract goal values that
generalize.


Minimal test: A bandit or grid-world task with delayed rewards, implemented twice. One network has an
“actor-critic” BG module (with a simulated DA signal and eligibility), the other has no such module (or no
eligibility). Both have ~60k weights. Measure cumulative reward or speed to reach criterion. A large
advantage for the BG-equipped network indicates its utility in credit assignment.


Confidence: High. Dopamine as RPE and BG actor-critic learning are deeply established (Schultz et al. 1997,
O’Reilly & Frank 2006【61†L115-L123】). The theoretical fit to RL is robust, though real circuit details
(striosomes, asynchronous activity) are complex. The three-factor rule in striatum has direct slice evidence
(Reynolds et al. 2001) and theoretical support (Izhikevich 2007【34†L1-L4】).

Entorhinal Grid Cells & Head-Direction Attractors. What it computes: Continuous spatial mapping and
integration. Head-direction (HD) cells form a ring-attractor that encodes the animal’s heading in allocentric
space (shown by single-unit recordings in thalamus and cortex; e.g. Taube 1990). Medial entorhinal cortex
(MEC) grid cells compute metric coordinates via path integration: as an animal moves, a population activity
bump shifts linearly, producing a hexagonal firing pattern (haTting et al. 2005). This is strongly shown by in
vivo recordings of stable grid fields across environments. Continuous-attractor network models (Burak &
Fiete 2009) capture how velocity shifts the bump.


Wiring diagram: The canonical model is a recurrent neural sheet. For HD: a one-dimensional ring of neurons
each tuned to a direction, with local excitatory and long-range inhibitory connections (Thomas et al., 1998
models). Inputs from angular velocity cells move the activity bump around the ring. For grid: a 2D sheet (or
multi-module rings) in layer II of MEC, with neurons (stellate/pyramidal) connected so that the population
activity forms a hexagonal attractor. Each cell connects to neighbors in the bump and receives velocity input
(from head-direction and speed cells). The sign is excitatory between similarly tuned cells, inhibitory for
distant ones. Convergence: each cell samples a dozen neighbors; divergence: each neighbor contacts ~10s
of cells. Noise is kept low by strong E–I balance.


Fixed vs plastic: The recurrent weights (E–I pattern forming the attractor) are assumed set by development.
Velocity-input synapses might be adaptive (Burgess 2008), but no explicit evidence. We can posit Hebbian
learning for coactive direction cells (landmark anchoring) but the main path-integration synapses likely use
a local update: Δw = η × pre_rate × post_rate for excitatory neighbors (classic Hopfield learning). A scalar
modulatory signal (e.g. feedback from visual cues) could gate any plasticity. Essentially, local STDP-like rules
could establish the Mexican-hat connectivity. The ring attractor is often treated as fixed connectivity.


Free parameters: For a ring of N neurons, fully specifying the weights is N^2, but practical connectivity is
sparse (each neuron connects ~k neighbors, so ~Nk). For N~100, ~10^4 synapses. A 2D grid network of
N~100×100 has ~10^4 neurons and perhaps ~10^6 synapses; but with sharing (each module repeating
same pattern) it’s O(10^3). In either case, parameters scale with the square of resolution. Rough estimate:
10^3–10^5 depending on sheet size and whether each synapse is independent.


Models: Numerous attractor models exist: Burak & Fiete (PLoS Comput Biol 2009) showed near-perfect path
integration. Its code is published. Couey et al. (Cell 2013) built a modular attractor model for rodent grid
cells. For HD, Zhang (1996) and others provided ring models. These reproduce grid alignment and HD
stability, but often assume idealized symmetry and neglect biological heterogeneity. Some fail to maintain
exact grids under noise unless specialized (Soman et al. 2016). Overall they capture spatial mapping well.


Benefit to a small learner: Multi-step computation and state representation. The attractor encodes
continuous variables persistently. An agent with a grid/HD module could do dead-reckoning: by
accumulating velocity in that space (vector addition) it can compute displacement to an unseen goal (as
ants do). This supports multi-hop planning and one-shot navigation rules. Mechanistically, recurrent
attractor activity holds latent state across steps, enabling integration without weight changes.


Minimal test: Train a network to navigate or path-integrate: e.g. simulate an agent moving in 1D with noisy
steps. Compare a recurrent ring network vs a feedforward baseline. Measure drift error after a sequence. A
lower error with the ring shows successful integration (HD property). Or for reuse: after learning to
compute step×k, test on step×(k+1) using the same attractor.




                                                       8
Confidence: High. The existence of continuous attractors for HD and grid coding is well-supported by
recordings (spatial periodicity in MEC【36†L1-L4】, and stable HD cells). The attractor model is strongly
inferred (global inhibition patterns observed). The main uncertainty is quantitative: biological connectivity
may not be perfect, and whether small circuits suffice.



Insect Central Complex (Compass & Vector Memory). What it computes: In insects, the central complex
(CX) implements an internal compass and vector navigation. The ellipsoid body (EB) contains a ring attractor
encoding heading (the “compass bump” of activity that tracks orientation) – shown by calcium imaging
(Seelig & Jayaraman 2015). The fan-shaped body (FB) and protocerebral bridge network encode goal
vectors: by combining heading with odometry signals, they form allocentric memory of a path segment (Lyu
et al. 2022). In effect, the CX adds vector addition to insect brains. Experimental evidence includes stable EB
activity as the animal walks in darkness【29†L238-L247】 and FB activity correlating with traveled distance
towards a goal (Green & Maimon, 2019).


Wiring diagram: A ring of ~16–64 “compass” neurons (E-PG/P-EN cells) spans the EB; these excite neighbors
and inhibit distant cells (via Delta7 GABA neurons) to maintain one activity bump. They interconnect via the
protocerebral bridge to other CX columns. Velocity and angular velocity signals (from leg/motor or optic
flow) project into this loop to shift the bump. The FB receives input from EB via columnar neurons; FB
neurons represent vector components (e.g. X and Y axes) and project back to EB or to memory loops. The
direction is excitatory; key interneurons (e.g. ring, pontine) implement WTA interactions. Convergence is
modular (tens of cells per column), divergence similar.


Fixed vs plastic: The compass ring connectivity seems largely fixed by development (genetically wired to
produce a ring attractor). However, learning occurs for landmarks: visual cues can calibrate the compass via
synapses from visual neurons to the ring【29†L243-L248】. The FB vectors may be updated via Hebbian-like
coincidence of heading and movement (evidence: explicit plasticity not yet shown, so assume minimal
synaptic changes). A plausible rule: Δw = η × pre_heading × pre_movement in the FB, gated by some
reinforcement of reaching the goal. But direct evidence is lacking, so local plasticity is “proposed”. The
MBON→DAN loop provides a reward-like signal that could gate CX learning.


Free parameters: The CX is compact: ~50 neurons in a ring and ~100 in FB (for fly). If each column-to-column
connection is a weight, parameters are hundreds (e.g. 50^2 ≈ 2500). Even with all interneurons, it’s roughly
10^2–10^3 parameters. For example, each EB column connects to a few neighbors (few weights per
neuron). Thus extremely small.


Models: Recent models by Xie et al. (Neuron 2021) and Stone et al. simulate CX path integration as vector
addition. Cuntz & Xygalatas (2022) coded a ring attractor for heading. Lyu & Maimon (2022, Nature)
provided a model for the allocentric vector computation in FB. These capture navigation behavior (goal-
directed steering, integration). They generally get the bump dynamics right; limitations include difficulty
fitting variability and biophysical constraints exactly.


Benefit to a small learner: This aids multi-step computation and rule reuse in spatial tasks. For example,
once the insect learns to go “north 10 steps, then east 5 steps,” the vector representation allows computing
a direct return route using vector algebra. The mechanism: the ring+vector memory lets the learner
accumulate and combine movement vectors without relearning from scratch each time. It would
particularly improve tasks requiring spatial inference (like accumulating modular sums analogously).



                                                       9
Minimal test: Compare networks on a 2D navigation task (e.g. find a hidden goal by path integration). One
network has a ring/V-vector module; the other uses only feedforward memory. Both trained to reach goals.
Measure final position error or ability to generalize to a new but related path. A clear advantage for the CX
circuit on novel combinations of moves supports its benefit.


Confidence: High. The CX head-direction bump is directly observed (Seelig & Jayaraman 2015). The vector-
memory proposal is also strongly backed by recent in vivo imaging (Lyu et al. 2022) showing allocentric
coordinate signals in the FB. The overall attractor nature and vector computations are well-documented in
insects.



Insect Mushroom-Body Output Loop (MBON–DAN Feedback). What it computes: Beyond serving as an
associative memory, the MB output circuit may implement something like a local error/prediction system.
Kenyon cells (KC) form sparse odor codes, MB output neurons (MBONs) read them, and dopaminergic
neurons (DANs) provide feedback (Aso et al. 2014 mapping). The loop suggests reward prediction: MBON
activity can influence DANs, adjusting future learning (inferred by connectomics【20†L11-L18】). APL
neurons mediate global inhibitory normalization on KCs (Attardo et al. 2015). Thus the MB+MBON+DAN
loop might predict unconditioned stimuli from odors (Bennett et al. 2021) in each compartment.


Wiring diagram: Each MB compartment has ~2000 KCs (excitatory) projecting divergently to ~5–10 MBONs
(mix of cholinergic excit/inhibitory)【29†L221-L230】. Each compartment has one or two DANs providing
dopamine to KC→MBON synapses. Remarkably, MBONs feed back to DANs of other compartments: for
example, MBON-γ1pedc (aversive output) excites appetitive DANs. The APL neuron (single per hemisphere)
receives from all KCs and broadcasts GABAergic inhibition back to KCs (roughly 1:1 connectivity). Ratio:
2000:10:1 (KCs:MBONs:DANs).


Fixed vs plastic: The critical plastic site is KC→MBON. Under dopamine, active KC→MBON synapses are
depressed (as shown by electrophysiology)【48†L149-L158】. We can write: Δw_kc-mbon = -η × kc_activity
× DAN_signal (only depresses). MBON→DAN or KC→APL are thought fixed. The APL inhibitory weight is
static, acting as normalization (non-plastic). Thus learning is local within each compartment, gated by the
compartment’s DAN. The MBON→DAN connections implement a fixed feedback (hardwired by
development).


Free parameters: About 2000 KCs × ~10 MBONs = ~2×10^4 synapses per compartment. With ~20
compartments, total ~4×10^5. After type-sharing (if each KC-MBON type shares a rule), still on the order of
10^5 independent weights. Because learning weakens, initial values matter. The primary dependency is KC
count and MBON count. In a compact fly model with ~10^3 KCs and ~5 outputs, it's ~5000 parameters.


Models: Bennett et al. (Nat Commun 2021) built a model in which the MBON-DAN loop encodes a prediction
error; they used it on a cue-switch task. Lipshutz et al. (PLOS Comput Biol 2023) proposed that MB learning
implements LDA (linear classifier). Both offer code or pseudocode. Their tasks include odor–reward
association; models capture learning asymmetries (e.g. only LTD) well, though they simplify many
anatomical details. Earlier models (Gibbons et al. 2003) used a delta rule on MBONs. These models get
associative learning in MB right; limitations include assumptions like a single DAN per valence.




                                                     10
Benefit to a small learner: Improves sample efficiency of associative learning and local credit assignment.
The MB enforces sparse representation (good generalization) and strong local error signals. For example, it
could learn associations between byte-patterns and rewards faster than plain backprop. The MBON→DAN
loop may also reduce interference: if one MBON fully predicts a reward, it can suppress further dopamine,
stabilizing learning (as in Bennett et al. 2021). It primarily serves reuse of class boundaries and efficient
reward-driven learning.


Minimal test: Train a network on a sparse classification task with reward feedback (e.g. classify odors to two
categories). Compare adding an MB-like sparse layer with DAN-gated plasticity vs a simple RNN. Measure
how many examples needed to reach performance. Improved learning speed suggests advantage.
Alternatively, test resistance to distractors (improved retention of learned associations).


Confidence: Medium. The anatomy of the MB and its loops is well-mapped (Aso et al. 2014; Eichler et al.
2017), and the phenomenology of one-shot learning and dopamine gating is established in insects.
However, exactly what the MBON–DAN feedback computes (prediction vs salience) is still being elucidated
(Bennett et al. 2021). The main uncertainties are in the precise local rule form and interactions between
compartments.



Sleep-Dependent Consolidation. What it computes: Offline restructuring of memory via replay and
homeostasis. During sleep, the brain reactivates prior experiences (hippocampal “replay” of place-cell
sequences【9†L68-L77】) and downscales synapses to renormalize weights (Tononi & Cirelli 2006). This is
shown by rodent and human studies: sequences of hippocampal spikes during rest match waking
experiences (Wilson & McNaughton 1994), and overall synaptic strengths are lower after sleep (Vyazovskiy
et al. 2008). The function is to consolidate salient memories (strengthen useful connections through replay)
while preventing runaway excitation (global weakening)【9†L68-L77】.


Wiring diagram: No new neurons, just existing circuits replaying patterns. Key players: hippocampus-cortex
loops active in slow-wave sleep; thalamocortical circuits generating spindles and slow oscillations. The
medial septum and raphe nuclei modulate global sleep states. Overall, every memory circuit is involved in
replay (hipp→cortex), and inhibitory circuits (e.g. slow-wave generation via cortical pyramidal–interneuron
loops) control downscaling. The “circuit” is the whole brain in a different state, not a distinct pathway.


Fixed vs plastic: During sleep, many synapses undergo global scaling: each weight is multiplied by a constant
<1 (activity-dependent scaling) – effectively plastic at whole-network scale. Locally, replaying specific
patterns drives LTP/LTD. We can describe two rules: (1) Global scaling: w ← w × (1 – δ) for all weights (δ
small). (2) Replay-driven Hebbian: for each pair of neurons coactive in a replayed sequence, Δw = η_replay ×
pre × post (potentiation). Dopaminergic or cholinergic tone modulates which synapses are consolidated
(evidence: high ACh blocks replay). Importantly, the local rules are just the normal STDP/LTP mechanisms,
but tagged by replay events.


Free parameters: No new weights added. The number of plastic synapses is as in the awake network (could
be 10^6+), but many are scaled automatically. Effective free “parameters” for consolidation are minimal
(global scale δ and LTP learning rate during replay). Thus O(1–10^1).




                                                      11
Models: “Synaptic homeostasis hypothesis” (Tononi & Cirelli, 2003) and biophysical models (DeMasa et al.
2018) propose frameworks. Royer & Paré (2003) modeled downscaling after LTP. McClelland et al. (1995)
incorporated consolidation dynamics in CLS models. Computational sleep-replay has been used to reduce
forgetting in neural nets (e.g. McClelland 1995, Kirkpatrick 2017 EWC though different). Zhou et al. 2026
(below) show offline replay helps structure learning. The models typically get improved retention; failures
include pathological overfitting if replay is unstructured.


Benefit to a small learner: Improves retention of old tasks and modest sample efficiency. By rehearsing
past inputs, the network reuses information without new rewards (potentially bolstering generalization to
unseen cases). Synaptic downscaling frees capacity and prevents saturation. Mechanistically, replay
interleaves experiences so that old knowledge isn’t erased by new learning; renormalization keeps weights
in a good range. For example, training a model on tasks sequentially with and without sleep phases would
likely show less forgetting with sleep.


Minimal test: After training on a task to criterion, compare two networks learning a second task: one gets
“sleep” (random replay of first-task patterns with weak learning) while the other learns continuously. Then
test performance on the first task. Better retention by the sleep network supports consolidation. Ensure
total parameters equal.


Confidence: Medium. There is abundant evidence for neural replay and homeostatic downscaling (Wilson &
McNaughton 1994; Vyazovskiy 2008), but linking these to improved generalization remains partly
theoretical. The exact balance of potentiation vs global weakening is debated (some argue “synaptic
renormalization” is oversimplified). It’s very plausible, but translating to small networks exactly is still a
hypothesis.



Thalamocortical Loops & Recurrence. What it computes: Multi-step and iterative computation via recurrent
cortical dynamics. Thalamus provides re-entrant connections among cortical areas: for example,
mediodorsal thalamus sustains prefrontal activity in working memory (Bolkan et al. 2017), and higher-order
thalamus gates specific cortical streams (Saalmann et al. 2012). Cortical layers exhibit heavy recurrence (A
and B dynamics). Functionally, these loops can perform attractor dynamics or generate intermediate
“mental rehearsal” steps. Experimental evidence (Pajevic & Plenz 2012; Bolkan 2017) shows thalamus is
needed for persistent cortical firing【20†L9-L18】; modeling proposals (Sherman & Guillery 2011) suggest
thalamus mediates cortical coupling.


Wiring diagram: Each cortical area receives bidirectional connections with specific thalamic nuclei. For
example, prefrontal L5 projects to mediodorsal (MD) thalamus (excitatory), and MD projects back to L3/L5
PFC. The reticular thalamic nucleus (RTN) provides global inhibition (inhibitory loops). Within cortex, L2/3-L5
recurrent loops exist (pyramidal–pyramidal excit). No easily cited convergence, but typical cortical
recurrence: each L2/3 neuron connects to ~100 nearby (Gilbert & Wiesel). Thalamic projections to cortex are
more focal, often a few hundred target neurons per thalamic cell.


Fixed vs plastic: Many thalamocortical synapses are plastic (LTP/LTD found in mediodorsal cortex after
learning) but plasticity is slower than cortical Hebb. Cortico-thalamic and thalamo-cortical may have gating
via neuromodulators (e.g. reticular bursts gating thalamus). A possible update: Δw = η × pre_cortex ×
post_thalamus when certain gating conditions (e.g. during learning tasks) are met. Within cortex, the
recurrence uses ordinary Hebbian/STDP rules. For simplicity, one might say LTP/LTD operates as usual on



                                                      12
recurrent synapses, modulated by behavioral state (rem vs REM sleep, etc.). No explicit “error” gating is
assumed; it’s unstructured Hebb.


Free parameters: If there are N cortical neurons and M thalamic, weights ~ N×M (thalamic afferents) + N^2
(cortical recurrence). For N=1000, that’s ~10^6. But in small model with 100+100, ~10^4–10^5. Each
thalamocortical pathway (e.g. MD→PFC) has maybe 10^4 synapses; corticothalamic similar. Overall 10^4–
10^6 depending on size.


Models: Friston’s predictive coding networks use cortical recurrence (Friston 2005). Rubin et al. (Neuron
2015) modeled cortical loops for temporal integration. Wimmer et al. (Nature Neurosci 2015) and Bolkan et
al. (2017) studied cortico-thalamic loops in PFC tasks. Lakshminarasimhan et al. (Cell Reports 2024)
simulated specific connectivity in thalamocortical loops to optimize learning. These models show loops can
sustain activity and implement gating, though they often simplify actual cell types. They correctly predict
that knocking out loops impairs multi-step tasks; a limitation is often lack of a clear learning algorithm
beyond Hebb.


Benefit to a small learner: Enables multi-step computation and inner rehearsal. A looped network can hold
intermediate results (working memory) and iterate through operations (like unrolling a computation). For
instance, a recurrence could simulate multi-layer inference via time. Mechanism: persistent activity (or
oscillatory dynamics) in the loop allows the network to “ponder” by feeding output back. This could partly
compensate for lack of explicit planning. However, without a clear local plasticity rule for iterative logic, the
benefit is qualitative.


Minimal test: Train a sequential inference task (e.g. computing parity by iterative stepwise XOR) with and
without recurrent loops. Both networks have ~60k weights. The looped model can recycle its hidden state
multiple times per input; measure accuracy after few “recurrent passes”. If it solves tasks requiring depth >1
that the feedforward model fails, loops are beneficial.


Confidence: Medium. Strong evidence (Bolkan et al. 2017) shows thalamic loops are crucial for PFC
maintenance. However, exactly how cortical recurrence is used for complex computation is less clear
experimentally. The concept is plausible (attractors, working memory) but bridging it to algorithmic benefits
(like planning) is theoretical.



Attention (Learned Routing in Cortex). What it computes: Selective gain modulation to route information
to the focus of processing. Attention effectively multiplies the gain of relevant neuron populations or
modulates synaptic efficacy (e.g. via cholinergic ACh or via thalamic pulvinar gating)【59†L1-L8】.
Biologically, attention is shown by increased firing and synchrony for attended stimuli (Fritz et al. 2007) and
by pulvinar's role in coordinating cortical areas (Saalmann 2012, Science: “pulvinar regulates cortical
transmission based on attention”【59†L1-L8】). Models propose that “attention keys” (learned signals) gate
a winner-take-all selection of inputs. Essentially it routes task-relevant features through selected channels.


Wiring diagram: In frontoparietal cortex, top-down feedback from areas like FEF/LIP projects to sensory
areas, synapsing on pyramidal and interneurons (gain change). Neuromodulatory centers (basal forebrain
for ACh, locus coeruleus for NE) broadcast to cortex to adjust global gain. The pulvinar thalamus receives
inputs from visual areas and FEF, and projects back to higher and lower cortical areas. A gating circuit (e.g.,




                                                       13
specialized Thalamic Reticular gating) can selectively inhibit or allow passage. The basic connection: a
biasing “gate” neuron inhibits a competing population, implemented by local interneurons or thalamic
gates. Convergence: Pulvinar cells receive from many cortical columns; divergence: each pulvinar axon
innervates ~hundreds of cortical neurons.


Fixed vs plastic: Synapses conveying attentional signals (e.g. FEF→V4) can undergo Hebbian/LTP (Moore et al.
2003). Learned routing implies these top-down weights adapt with experience. We might write: Δw_top-
down = η × pre_attention_signal × post_sensory_activity. Neuromodulator-driven gain is a scalar
modulator (ACh) adjusting effective weights in real-time (not via synaptic change). Winner-take-all selection
could use plastic lateral inhibition among columns (e.g. cortical interneurons adapting to emphasize some
inputs). Overall, plasticity is task-driven (reinforced by reward of focusing correctly) but specifics are unclear.
A plausible local rule: if an attended input repeatedly predicts reward, its cortical synapses are strengthened
by DA.


Free parameters: Depends on the number of attentional sources and targets. If M attention “gates” (e.g.
features) and N sensory units, weights ~ M×N. For a few dozen possible attention channels and hundreds of
targets, parameters ~10^3–10^4. Also include gain factors per cortical neuron (~N). Order 10^3.


Models: Many AI and neuroscience models: Mnih et al. 2014 (attention nets), Hamker 2011 (Biol Cybern). For
brain, O’Reilly et al. (J CogNeuro 1993) modeled frontal gating. Saalmann et al. 2012 gave physiological data;
Buschman & Miller 2007 (PNAS) showed oscillatory gating in PFC. More recently, Lisman & Jensen (2013)
proposed theta-gamma coding for WTA selection. Models often capture improved selection of relevant
features; some fail to explain fast switching between tasks or why certain cells act as “keys.”


Benefit to a small learner: Enhances sample efficiency and rule reuse by focusing computation on task-
relevant subspaces. For instance, learning to apply a rule only to highlighted features reduces interference
by irrelevant inputs. Mechanism: attention amplifies or routes specific neural activations so that the learner
effectively solves a simpler problem (less noisy input). It also approximates a learned gating function (like
using feature-specific weights). One could also consider it enabling multitasking. However, it requires some
supervisory signal (reward or instruction) to learn the routing.


Minimal test: Train on a multi-feature discrimination where only one feature is predictive. Compare a net
with a learnable attention-gate (e.g. a multiplicative mask on inputs) vs a standard net. Both have ~60k
weights. After training, test on variations where the predictive feature is new. The attention-net should zero
in on the correct dimension faster (fewer samples). Measure classification accuracy vs number of trials.


Confidence: Medium. There is solid physiological evidence that attention alters gain and mediates routing
(Saalmann 2012【59†L1-L8】). But the mechanism of “learned keys” (how the network discovers what to
attend) is mostly theoretical. Attention in biology is a complex mix of bottom-up and top-down signals, and
our abstraction as a learned routing structure is plausible but simplified.

Each circuit is judged by evidence (strongest experimental support), parsimony of parameters, presence of
local plasticity rules, and how costly the minimal test would be. Insects’ central complex and MB loop lead
by having very compact, well-mapped circuits and known plasticity (thus high evidence and few
parameters). Classical mammalian circuits follow; models of cortical feedback and attention are promising
but less substantiated, and require large parameter counts.
