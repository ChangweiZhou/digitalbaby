The terms of the description (cell outside the encoder, encoder cell, arrival, prediction, mismatch, step, strength, neighbourhood) are used here as the description defines them. The following further terms are used.

- A *synapse* is a single point of contact at which one cell's output acts on another cell. A *connection* from cell A to cell B is the set of all synapses from A onto B; in the cortex of mammals a connection between two pyramidal cells usually consists of several synapses.
- A *dendrite* is a receiving branch of a nerve cell; a *dendritic spine* is a small protrusion of a dendrite that carries one excitatory synapse; the *axon* is the single output fibre of a cell, which branches to reach all of the cell's targets.
- *Dale's principle* is the textbook finding that a nerve cell releases the same transmitter substances at all of its output synapses, so that its direct effect on its targets is either excitatory or inhibitory and does not change with learning.
- *Homeostatic plasticity* is a change in a cell's synapses or excitability, driven by the cell's own activity over hours to days, that returns that activity toward a level typical of the cell. *Synaptic scaling* is the form in which all of a cell's excitatory input strengths are multiplied by a common factor; *intrinsic plasticity* is the form in which the cell's own excitability (its threshold or gain) changes.
- An *eligibility trace* is a quantity held at a synapse that records recent coincident activity for a period of seconds, so that a later signal can still change that synapse.
- *Reafference* is the input an organism receives as a consequence of its own action, for example the sound of its own voice.
- *Replay* is the reactivation, by the tissue itself during rest, of sequences of activity that occurred during recent experience; in *reverse replay* the sequence is reactivated in reverse order.

A sentence *passes* if its mark is right (for [S], the words are in sections 7 to 10; for [F], it follows by necessity from those words and from earlier sentences; for [C], the matter is really open under Mr. Xu's rules, including the rule that nothing may be proposed that a living cell or tissue cannot do) and if a living nerve cell or tissue can do or have what it says. A sentence *fails* if one of these is clearly not so. A sentence is *doubtful* if its mark or its biology is open to a specific objection that does not make it false, or if the science is unsettled. ### 1.2 Sentences that fail

**2 [S] (two kinds of cell).** The statement of section 8 sets the byte-level encoder apart, and section 10 adds an output end whose cells also learn. The tissue therefore has at least three positions for a cell (at the input end, at the output end, and between them), and the sentence's division into two kinds is superseded by section 10. In addition, the Chinese "不同的读到的不一样" ("different ones read different things") does not say that the encoder consists of nerve cells; the words "one of its cells" are the translation's. That the encoder consists of cells that learn follows only from section 10. Correction: "There are cells at the input end (encoder cells), cells at the output end, and cells between them; all are nerve cells, and those at both ends learn (section 10)."

**11 [C] (fixed sign of a cell, choice C3).** The matter is not open under the rule that nothing may be proposed that a living cell cannot do. By Dale's principle a living cell's direct effect on its targets is either excitatory or inhibitory, and learning changes the strength of a synapse but not its sign. The known exceptions (release of more than one transmitter, the depolarising action of the inhibitory transmitter GABA early in development) do not make the sign learnable. The form "no fixed sign" of C3 is therefore excluded, and only the share of inhibitory cells remains a choice. Correction: mark [F] from the rule on living cells, with the share of inhibitory cells as the remaining choice.

**12 [C] (negative outputs, choice C4).** A living cell's output to other cells is its firing (or, for cells without action potentials, its release of transmitter), which cannot fall below zero. A cell with a spontaneous rate can signal a decrease below that rate, but the decrease is bounded by the rate. The form "outputs of either sign" is excluded by the same rule. Correction: mark [F]: "A cell's output is not below zero; a decrease can be signalled only as a fall below the cell's own ongoing rate."

**28 [C] (same output to all targets, choice C9).** A living cell has one axon, and an action potential travels into all its branches; the cell cannot send one signal to one target and another to a second. What differs between targets is the receiving synapse (its strength and its short-term facilitation or depression), which belongs to the connection, not to the sending cell's output. Failure of conduction at branch points is reported under some conditions; whether it is used in normal function is unsettled, and it would in any case not let a cell choose a different signal for each target. The form "different outputs to different targets" is excluded. Correction: mark [F]: "A cell sends the same output to every cell it is connected to."

**57 [F] (nothing about unconnected cells).** The conclusion does not follow. By section 9 a cell may release a chemical signal into a region near it, and by sentence 52 a cell in that region receives it whether or not the two are connected. A cell can therefore hold a quantity that depends on the activity of nearby cells to which it is not connected. Living tissue uses exactly this. Glutamate released by active axons reaches nearby dendrites beyond the synapse itself (spillover), and glutamate applied at a point near a dendrite causes a new spine to grow there within tens of seconds (Kwon and Sabatini, 2011). Neurotrophic factors such as BDNF are released in an activity-dependent manner and promote local growth and stabilisation of synapses. Correction: "A cell receives nothing through a connection from a cell it is not connected to; it can receive, through the chemical signal of its region, a quantity that depends on the activity of nearby cells, and the choice of a new source may rest on that quantity." Choice C18 must then gain a form that uses it (section 4).

**69 [C] (whether encoder cells adjust and predict, choice C22).** Section 10 rules that the cells at the input end learn how to take in through learning. The first form of C22, "fixed and not predicting", is excluded. Whether encoder cells also predict depends on whether their learning is the same local learning as that of other cells; section 10's Chinese ("这两个也需要通过学习来学会如何输入/输出", "these two also need to learn, through learning, how to take in and give out") does not say "the same", which is the reviewer's reading. Correction: "[F] Encoder cells adjust their connections (section 10). [C] Whether they also predict their arrivals is choice C22."

**92 [F] (no part of the tissue is its output).** Section 10 rules that there is an output end. The first half of the sentence is therefore false. The second half (no cell reads every cell) still follows. Correction: "No cell reads every cell; the output end is a set of cells like the others, which learn (sentences 101 to 121)."

**94 [F] (no pass over an item after it has ended).** The reason given (no cell reads past bytes; nothing marks the end of an item) excludes a pass imposed by a program on the stored bytes, but it does not exclude a pass made by the tissue itself over its own recent activity. Living tissue performs such passes: in the hippocampus, sequences of activity from recent experience are reactivated during rest, in forward and in reverse order (Foster and Wilson, 2006). Section 3.4 of the reviewer's draft lists this as the organism's own act, and the roadmap accepts "the tissue's own repetition". Correction: "No program passes over an item, or over stored bytes, after it has ended; the tissue's own reactivation of recent activity is not excluded and is not imposed."

### 1.3 Sentences that are doubtful

**3 [S] (three things and no more).** The words are Mr. Xu's ("只是", "only"). But section 7, point 2 and section 9 (also Mr. Xu's) add that a cell may release a chemical signal, and sentence 32 records this. Read literally, "no more" contradicts sentence 32. The sentence should say: "three things, and in addition it may release a chemical signal into its region (sentence 32)".

**5 [F] (stored state is the only record of the past).** The cell's connections and their strengths are also a record of the past; in living tissue they are the main long-term record. The sentence follows only if "state" includes the cell's connections and strengths. This must be said, since sentences 38 and 43 rely on it.

**9 [S] (the gated cell).** The mark is right: Mr. Xu accepted it (section 7, point 1). Whether a living cell has a counterpart of three internal gates, each a weighted sum of the cell's inputs, that control input, retention and output, is unsettled. Parts of it have counterparts: multiplicative interaction of inputs on a dendrite, shunting inhibition, and persistent firing sustained by currents inside the cell. A complete set of three such gates is not established.

**14 [S] (acts and adjusts only within its own neighbourhood).** The words are in section 9 as a report of Mr. Xu's statement of 16 November ("a neuron acts and adjusts only within its own neighbourhood"); the description quotes them with "cell" for "neuron", which is acceptable. The biology depends on what a neighbourhood is. If it is the set of cells near the cell in space, the sentence is false of many living cells: a pyramidal cell of the cortex sends its axon to other areas several millimetres or centimetres away. If it is the set of cells that the cell's axon and dendrites come close to, wherever they are, it is true, and synapse formation and chemical release are both local in that sense. Choice C30 must be read in the second sense.

**23 [C] (graded strength or present/absent, choice C5).** The choice is formally open, but two points must be added. First, the Chinese "不断调整自己连接那些细胞" can be read as "keep adjusting which cells it connects to" or as "keep adjusting its connections to those cells"; the sentence's "strict reading is a set of cells without strengths" is therefore not the only strict reading. Second, in living tissue a connection between two cells is graded (number of synapses, probability of release, number of receptors). Whether a single synapse has only a few discrete states is unsettled (Petersen and colleagues, 1998; O'Connor and colleagues, 2005). The form "present or absent only" corresponds to a living connection only if a connection is a single synapse, or if its strength is counted in synapses.

**29 [F] (nothing sent backward).** It is correct that a cell has no second kind of electrical output. But the sentence says "sends nothing backward", and the chemical signal of sentence 32 reaches the sending cells' terminals in the region. Living cells do send chemical signals backward across their own synapses: endocannabinoids released by the receiving cell suppress release from the sending terminal (Wilson and Nicoll, 2001), and nitric oxide acts similarly. How specific to single synapses these signals are is unsettled. Two further living facts bear on this: electrical synapses (gap junctions) pass the membrane potential in both directions, and they would be two connections in a loop under sentence 30. The sentence should say: "A cell sends no electrical output backward; what reaches the sending cells from it is its ordinary output, if they are among its targets, and its chemical signal, if they lie in its region."

**34 [S] (not the next byte).** The Chinese reads "不能简单认为是'预期下一个到来的字节'", that is, "cannot simply be taken as 'expecting the next byte to arrive'". The word "simply" is missing in the translation and the sentence. What is denied is that a cell's task is simply byte expectation. For an encoder cell, or for a cell whose arrivals come mostly from encoder cells, predicting the arrival includes predicting parts of the next byte.

**35 [F] (the prediction exists before the arrival).** The necessity holds if prediction is meant in time, which is the natural reading of "新来的信息" (newly arriving information). But the second form of choice C13 (sentence 46) predicts the inputs at one part of the cell from the inputs arriving at another part, possibly at the same moment. The two sentences are then inconsistent. Either sentence 46 is read as "the inputs arriving at one part predict the inputs that will arrive at another part", or sentence 35 must admit a prediction formed from simultaneous arrivals at another part.

**38 [F] (information kept in states and outputs).** Short-term changes of synaptic strength (facilitation that lasts hundreds of milliseconds to seconds) are a textbook property of living synapses, and a role for them in holding recent information has been proposed (Mongillo, Barak and Tsodyks, 2008; whether the brain uses it this way is unsettled). The phrase "the statement offers no other place" is right only if a cell's state includes the short-term state of its synapses. This should be said.

**44 [F] (no other cell receives the mismatch).** If a cell releases its chemical signal on an occasion defined by its mismatch (sentence 82, choice C26), a function of its mismatch reaches the cells of its region. The sentence should add "or the chemical signal, if its release depends on the mismatch".

**46 [C] (what predicts what, choice C13).** Doubtful for the inconsistency with sentence 35 described above. The biology of the two-part form is unsettled: in a cortical pyramidal cell, inputs to the distal (apical) dendrite and to the proximal (basal) dendrite interact, and their coincidence produces bursts of firing (Larkum, Zhu and Sakmann, 1999); that this interaction is a comparison of a prediction with an arrival is a hypothesis.

**47 [C] (where each quantity lies).** The sentence itself says that this "follows from choice C13". It is a consequence of C13, not a further open matter, and should be marked [F] conditional on C13.

**53 [C] (how a strength changes, choice C15).** The choice is open, and both forms named have living counterparts: the coincidence form is long-term potentiation as found in many synapses; a form in which the change depends on the receiving cell's output being lower or higher than a learned expectation exists in cerebellum-like structures, where synapses weaken when sending and receiving activity coincide, which makes the cell's response cancel predictable input (Bell and colleagues, 1997, in the electrosensory lobe of mormyrid fish). The doubt is that the sentence lets a strength change without bound and in either direction. A living synapse cannot become negative (sentence 11 as corrected) and has a maximum. The sentence should add: "a strength stays between zero and a maximum".

**60 [C] (incoming or outgoing connections, choice C19).** The choice is open, but the reason given ("a cell holds quantities only about its incoming connections") is not right of living cells. The sending terminal of a synapse belongs to the sending cell and holds local quantities (its recent release, its calcium, retrograde chemical signals); axonal boutons, like spines, are formed and removed in the adult cortex. The simplest form may still be "incoming only", but the reason should be that it is simplest, not that it is the only form a cell has quantities for.

**75 [F] (nothing resets states).** It follows that no agent outside the cells resets them. It does not follow that a cell's state is never returned toward a starting value at the end of an item: a cell may come, by its own adjustment, to clear its state when particular bytes arrive (for example the sign = or an end mark), which is what a learned gate on retention does. The roadmap's condition 4.2 allows the bytes themselves to act as marks. The sentence should say "nothing outside the cells".

## 3. Task (c): the four gaps and the reviewer's proposed answers

### Gap 1. Nothing prevents the tissue from making prediction trivial

**What living tissue does.** (1) Homeostatic plasticity: when the activity of cultured cortical cells is blocked for two days, the strengths of all their excitatory inputs are scaled up by a common factor, and when activity is raised they are scaled down (Turrigiano and colleagues, 1998); the excitability of the cell itself also changes in the direction that restores its activity (Desai, Rutherford and Turrigiano, 1999). (2) The threshold that divides strengthening from weakening of a synapse moves with the cell's recent activity: after a period without visual input, synapses in visual cortex strengthen more readily (Kirkwood, Rioult and Bear, 1996), as predicted by the theory of Bienenstock, Cooper and Munro (1982). (3) Cells respond less to a repeated or predictable stimulus and more to a rare one (Ulanovsky, Las and Nelken, 2003). (4) The total size of a cell's excitatory synapses on a stretch of dendrite is held roughly constant while individual synapses grow and shrink (Bourne and Harris, 2011), so that one synapse grows at the expense of others.

**Does this fill the gap with quantities a cell has?** In part. Homeostatic plasticity uses only the cell's own activity and is textbook; it prevents a cell from falling silent. A movable threshold (2) uses only the cell's own activity and makes a constantly active input weaken as readily as strengthen. Competition among a cell's own synapses (4) forces a cell to keep some inputs strong. None of these prevents a cell from firing at its usual rate in a way that carries nothing about the bytes, and none prevents a cell from keeping the sources that are easiest to predict. A rule by which a cell keeps the whole distribution of its own activity, not only its mean, near a target would push its activity to vary; it has been proposed and modelled (Stemmler and Koch, 1999; Triesch, 2005), but whether living cells do this is unsettled.

**What remains open.** Whether a cell that predicts its arrivals and keeps its own mean activity also keeps its output informative about the bytes. One fact limits the gap: the input lines are driven by the bytes, and no cell can silence them; the trivial outcome can occur only in cells away from the input end. This is the first question of step 1 of the roadmap.

**The reviewer's answer** ("a cell regulates its own level of activity, so cells do not fall silent"): **incomplete.** It is right that a living cell regulates its own level of activity from its own quantities. But gap 1 concerns outputs that are constant or uninformative as well as silent, and the description itself had already said (gap 1) that C29 changes how fast the trivial outcome is reached but does not exclude it. The answer must add the forms that act against constancy (the movable threshold, competition among a cell's synapses) and must state that whether these suffice is to be read at step 1.

### Gap 2. Nothing makes a cell's prediction matter to any other cell

**What living tissue does.** In cerebellum-like structures, the principal cells learn, by the synaptic rule of sentence 53, an inhibitory input that matches the predictable part of their sensory input, so that what they send on is the input minus its predictable part (Bell and colleagues, 1997). Thus another cell receives what was not predicted. In the visual cortex of the mouse, some cells respond when visual flow differs from what the animal's own running would produce (Keller, Bonhoeffer and Hübener, 2012). The subtraction is done at the membrane: an excitatory arrival and an inhibitory input that carries the prediction sum at the cell, which a living cell can do.

**Does this fill the gap with quantities a cell has?** In part. If the cell's output carries its mismatch (choice C10, second or third form), then the arrivals of the cells it sends to depend on how well it predicts, and its prediction matters to them. A living cell can compute this from its own quantities. But it does not by itself divide work among cells, and it acts against gap 1: a cell that predicts perfectly sends nothing, so the cells after it receive nothing. A signed mismatch cannot be sent by one living cell as such (sentence 12 as corrected); it needs two kinds of cell, one for arrivals above the prediction and one for arrivals below, or a rate above zero around which the cell varies.

**What remains open.** Whether passing on what was not predicted, together with homeostatic plasticity, leads cells away from the input end to carry information about earlier bytes; and whether the reverse direction (later cells informing the prediction of earlier cells through reciprocal connections, sentence 30) is needed for cells to divide work.

**The reviewer's answer** ("a cell passes on what is new to it, which is Mr. Xu's own wording ('new information'), so one cell's prediction matters to the next"): **its reason is wrong, and the proposal is incomplete.** Mr. Xu's words are "通过其他细胞得到的新的信息" and "预测从其他细胞得来的新来的信息": information newly arriving through other cells, set against the "state stored in the past". "新" here means newly arrived in time, not unpredicted by the cell. Mr. Xu's statement therefore does not say that a cell passes on what it did not predict; that is choice C10 and must be marked as a choice, not as his wording. As a choice it is one that living tissue makes in some circuits, and it does make one cell's prediction matter to the next; but it worsens gap 1, and it requires the sign of the mismatch to be carried by two kinds of cell.

### Gap 3. The task is only more bytes, and the tissue makes no attempt

**What living tissue does.** Animals act and sense the consequences of their action, including their own actions (reafference). In classical conditioning, an animal comes to produce a response before a stimulus that at first followed it; in eyeblink conditioning the change is in identified cells of the cerebellum and depends on a path by which the later stimulus drives those cells. Songbirds learn to sing by producing and hearing their own song and comparing it with a memory of a tutor's song; in this case a signal that rises and falls with how close the song is to the model is carried by dopamine cells to one region (Gadagkar and colleagues, 2016). That signal is local to a region but is formed from a comparison with a model, and the reader is unsure whether its counterpart is permitted under Mr. Xu's rule that no cell receives an error computed for it; this check does not use it.

**Does this fill the gap with quantities a cell has?** Section 10 fills the part of gap 3 that says the tissue acts on nothing: there is an output end, so there is an attempt. It does not fill the part that asks why the tissue should come to produce the answer digits, and sentence 119 shows that a cell at the output end has nothing that tells it whether the attempt was right. Living tissue learns an anticipatory response from quantities cells have, but only when the later stimulus already reaches the responding cells (condition (i) of sentence 119).

**What remains open.** Condition (i) of sentence 119: how the shown answer comes to drive the output cells for the same byte, by a path present at the start or by the tissue's own emissions read back (choices C35 and C36); and why, among all bytes, the tissue should come to produce the answer digits. This is the further gap numbered 5 in the summary.

**The reviewer's answer** ("the tissue emits bytes and the right answer follows as input, so there is an attempt"): **incomplete.** That there is an attempt is right, by section 10. But the answer passes over the first half of gap 3 and over the question of how anything relates the attempt to the answer that follows; by the statement alone nothing does (sentence 119).

### Gap 4. A new connection can be made only blindly

**What living tissue does.** In the adult cortex dendritic spines are continually formed and removed; most new spines last a few days, a minority become lasting, and learning a new task raises both formation and the lasting of new spines (Trachtenberg and colleagues, 2002; Holtmaat and colleagues, 2005; Xu and colleagues, 2009; Yang, Pan and Gan, 2009). Formation is not blind: glutamate released near a dendrite causes a new spine to grow toward the release site (Kwon and Sabatini, 2011); the candidates for a new synapse are the axons that already pass within a few micrometres of the dendrite (Stepanyants, Hof and Chklovskii, 2002); and neurotrophic factors released by active cells promote growth and stabilisation nearby.

**Does this fill the gap with quantities a cell has?** Yes, in part, and it overturns sentence 57. A cell can grow a new connection toward a nearby site where transmitter or another chemical signal from active axons coincides with its own activity; both quantities are present at the cell. This is permitted by section 9, since the signal is local. In a simulation it requires that each cell can read, at its own sites, a local quantity formed from the activity of nearby cells to which it is not connected; this is a sum over nearby cells formed by the medium, as sentence 83 already says of the chemical signal.

**What remains open.** Whether formation guided in this way, together with removal of connections that stay weak, is enough for information to be relayed over several connections from the input lines to the output end; and how large the region must be.

**The reviewer's answer** ("connections are tried at random within the neighbourhood and kept or given up by use, and whether that suffices is answered at step 2"): **incomplete.** Random trial followed by keeping or giving up is a living form (spine turnover) and is a reasonable first form. But the answer accepts sentence 57, which is wrong: living tissue does not make new connections only blindly, and the form guided by local activity is available under Mr. Xu's rulings. "Use" must also be defined in the cell's own quantities (choice C17). The question whether it suffices belongs to step 1 as much as to step 2, since step 1 already asks whether information reaches cells away from the input lines.
